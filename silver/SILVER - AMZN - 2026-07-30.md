# AMZN — fiscal 2026-Q2 call (2026-07-30)

**The key idea:** AWS just posted its fifth consecutive quarter of accelerating growth (36.7%, an 18-quarter high) while *expanding* margin to 39% — the opposite of what the Street expected from an AI-heavy capex cycle — and management responded by raising 2026 CapEx guidance again, to $220B, explicitly because of memory-price inflation. The tension: Amazon insists this is disciplined, contracted, break-even-tested spending against booked demand ($496B backlog, 2028 capacity already reserved), but it's also the same quarter they had to raise the number for a cost reason outside their control.

## The read — 3-5 points from the whole transcript
1. **AWS reaccelerated into an 18-quarter high growth rate and expanded margin doing it.** Revenue grew 36.7% YoY, adding "$4.6 billion in revenue quarter-over-quarter, about 80% more than our largest increase ever," while operating margin hit 39% (up 650bps YoY, 520bps ex-derivative gain) — directly rebutting the analyst premise that AI workloads would be structurally lower-margin.
2. **CapEx guidance got raised mid-year for a supply cost reason, not a demand reason.** The 2026 number moved from ~$200B to ~$220B, and Jassy was explicit: "The higher cost of memory pushing this number up from our prior estimate." This is scarcity showing up as a cost line, not a demand signal.
3. **Amazon is quietly hedging its "no frontier model needed" thesis.** Jassy spent several minutes arguing Bedrock's model-agnostic breadth is the real moat ("there is not going to be one model to rule the world") — then confirmed Amazon is building its own frontier model anyway, for cost control, prioritization, and speed. Both things are said in the same breath without acknowledging the tension.
4. **The backlog conversation implies capacity, not demand, is now the multi-year ceiling.** RPO is "two and a half times" the year-ago quarter; capacity for 2027 is "largely reserved" and 2028 demand is already "striking." Jassy reframed AWS's addressable ceiling from "a few hundred billion" to potentially "a trillion-dollar annual revenue business."
5. **Grocery/perishables finally found a working format after years of experiments.** Same-day perishables in 2,300 U.S. cities are producing "nine of the top 10 best sellers" locally and a 50%+ jump in monthly active perishables customers — a genuine inflection Jassy calls out as distinct from years of prior grocery trial-and-error.

## Economy & consumer
- **No demand-side warning signs surfaced.** Management attributes cost pressure to fuel inflation ("driven by fuel inflation from the conflict in the Middle East") and "driver capacity limitations," not softening consumer spend.
- **Tariffs were mostly absorbed, not passed through.** Amazon received ~$600M in tariff refunds but calls the amount "limited" since Amazon isn't the importer of record for most goods sold and "largely absorbed these costs rather than pass them on to customers."
- **Value-shopping behavior is strong.** Prime Day saw "more than 80% at our lowest price of the year," and Haul's sub-$10 selection expanded "nearly 20x since launch" — consistent with a consumer still very price-sensitive.
- **No commentary on broader employment, wage, or discretionary-spending trends** was offered — notable given Amazon's size as a bellwether; the omission itself is a data point that this call is AWS-led, not consumer-led.

## The business — what's working, what's not
- **AWS is carrying the entire growth story.** $169B annualized run rate, AI revenue run rate "over $25 billion" and chips revenue run rate also "over $25 billion," both growing "triple-digit percentages year-over-year."
- **Core cloud and AI are reinforcing each other, per Jassy's framing:** "growth in one is driving growth in the other," because post-training/RL/agentic tool use runs on CPU (Graviton), pulling core consumption along with AI adoption — a structural argument for why AWS growth compounds rather than cannibalizes.
- **Retail margins are steady but unspectacular:** North America operating margin 7.9%, International 4.1% — solid execution (robotics fleet doubling, shorter shipping distances) but overshadowed by AWS this quarter.
- **Fulfillment costs are being managed against real headwinds:** "we continue to lower our overall cost to serve, even as we face heightened transportation costs," offset partly by an April FBA fuel/logistics surcharge — a direct pass-through mechanism worth tracking.

## Investing & scarcity
- **CapEx raised again to ~$220B for 2026**, attributed explicitly to memory cost inflation rather than a change in demand outlook.
- **Capacity, not demand, is now the stated bottleneck through 2027:** "we will still not have enough capacity to meet all the demand we have in 2026, and I believe this dynamic will also be true in 2027, too."
- **Component-level scarcity is now priced into new contracts:** Jassy acknowledged "inflated prices right now on some of the components like memory and hard drives and SSDs," with new agreements built to account for it — existing signed contracts are locked at prior pricing.
- **Debt issuance has entered the funding mix:** "You've seen us issue debt this year," with Olsavsky declining to specify future capital-sourcing plans beyond "we'll continue to look at all the options."
- **Power capacity build is on track:** reaffirmed plan to "have double the power capacity by the end of 2027 that we had in 2025."

## Where they think it's going vs what they're doing about it
- **Belief:** AWS could become "a trillion-dollar annual revenue business... in time." **Action:** CapEx raised to $220B and data-center commitments locked in years ahead of monetization — the two-year-lead-time data center cycle means today's spend is a bet on demand that won't be fully tested for years.
- **Belief:** "There is not going to be one model to rule the world" and Bedrock's multi-model neutrality is the durable moat. **Action:** Simultaneously building an in-house frontier model "for a few reasons" including cost control and prioritization — a direct hedge against the very thesis just stated, acknowledged only implicitly.
- **Belief:** Enterprise AI adoption is still in early, "barbelled" stages, with the large middle segment (existing production workloads) barely tapped. **Action:** capacity for 2028 already reserved and Trainium multi-gigawatt commitments locked in from Anthropic and OpenAI — betting the CapEx now on demand described as still nascent.
- **Belief:** Third-party Trainium sales are a real future opportunity ("increasing number of customers... interested"). **Action:** no committed timeline, decision, or pilot announced — still "actively having those conversations."

## Hedges — what they wouldn't commit to
- **No timeline on selling Trainium to third parties:** "I expect there's a real chance we'll do that in the future" — optionality language, not a commitment.
- **No specifics on future capital sourcing** beyond confirming debt issuance already happened this year; Colin Sebastian's direct question on funding sources got a non-answer ("nothing to share today").
- **Declined to fully commit AWS's identity to a proprietary frontier model**, framing it as one of "at least a half dozen" comparable models rather than a strategic centerpiece — while still funding its development.
- **No numeric guidance on 2027/2028 capacity build** beyond qualitative reassurance that the 2027 power-doubling plan is "on track" and that "the lion's share of capacity in 2027... is largely reserved."

## The street — what analysts asked
- **Margin durability dominated the Q&A.** The opening question directly challenged the assumption that AI workloads compress margin — AWS's 39% margin beat that framing, and Olsavsky's answer leaned on "disciplined efficiency gains, capacity optimization... and always closely managing our fixed costs" rather than a structural explanation, leaving open whether Q2's capacity-optimization benefit repeats.
- **Capacity-versus-backlog questions recurred across multiple analysts** (Justin Post, Brian Nowak, Ken Gawrelski) — all probing how much of the RPO/backlog surge is real booked demand versus optimistic framing, and whether long-lived data center spend can ever slow down given 2027-28 is already largely reserved.
- **Supply-chain cost inflation on components (memory, SSDs) was raised directly** in the context of whether long-term AWS contracts protect return profiles — management's answer was reassuring but non-quantitative ("new agreements... take into account what your costs are").
- **The Trainium third-party sale question tested strategic ambition** beyond AWS's own cloud — met with an openly non-committal answer.
- **Compressed worry:** can AWS's margin and ROIC hold up once today's capacity-optimization tailwind fades, memory/component inflation works fully through new contracts, and the massive 2027-28 capacity build — largely pre-committed rather than demand-confirmed in real time — actually gets utilized?
