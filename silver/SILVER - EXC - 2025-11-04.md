# EXC — fiscal 2025-Q3 call (2025-11-04)

**The key idea:** Exelon's quarter itself is noise — a weather-and-timing beat inside a reaffirmed guidance band — but the real story is a regulated monopoly positioning itself as the answer to an AI-driven power shortage it insists competitive markets can't solve alone. The tension: management wants states to hand utilities more control (rate-based generation, mandatory TSAs, "all of the above" solutions) precisely at the moment competitors like Constellation are stepping up with their own merchant proposals, and Exelon is characterizing those competing responses as insufficient.

## The read — 3-5 points from the whole transcript
1. **The beat was weather, not operations.** The $0.15 y/y EPS improvement broke down to $0.12 from higher distribution/transmission rates and $0.06 from favorable storm/deferral timing at PECO and BGE — "ahead of the expectations noted in our prior quarter call, primarily due to better-than-normal storm conditions, timing of O&M spend and tax timing." This is a good quarter engineered by rate design and luck, not a sign of accelerating fundamentals.
2. **Large-load pipeline discipline is the real product being sold to investors.** Exelon built a two-step gate (cluster study, then signed Transmission Security Agreement) explicitly to counter "a lot of double counting that may be taking place across the country," per Calvin Butler — an implicit shot at peers reporting inflated data-center pipelines. Only 19GW clears that bar despite a 47GW headline pipeline.
3. **Exelon is lobbying to become the generation owner of last resort.** Butler repeatedly pushed "utility-owned generation that regulators oversee" as the fix for supply shortfalls, arguing markets alone can't be trusted — a direct pivot away from Exelon's post-spinoff pure-wires identity back toward owning generation.
4. **TSAs are the new customer-protection mechanism for large load.** ComEd has now filed a large-load tariff requiring TSAs for all loads over 50MW, mirroring the PECO/Amazon-adjacent template, designed so speculative data-center demand that never materializes doesn't get socialized onto existing ratepayers.
5. **Guidance discipline stays rigid.** Nothing from the $10-15B transmission opportunity set or PJM open-window bids enters the plan until "certain and bankable" — CFO Jeanne Jones: "we're not going to put anything in the plan that isn't certain and bankable."

## Economy & consumer
- **No macro cycle exposure, but affordability politics dominate.** As a regulated utility, EXC's "economy" read is really about ratepayer tolerance for rising bills amid AI-driven demand growth — Butler acknowledges customers "have voiced very strongly that they're frustrated with high energy costs."
- **Consumer relief is programmatic, not discretionary.** LIHEAP support and new rate-design offerings for "customers who need it most" are the only consumer-facing lever; there's no read on broader household financial health since this isn't a market-facing consumer business.

## The business — what's working, what's not
- **Reliability performance keeps improving.** All four operating utilities ranked 1, 2, 4, 7 among peers, up from 1, 3, 5, 8 last year — a genuine operational strength repeatedly tied to earning "the right" to rate increases.
- **Rate case backlog is a mixed bag.** Delmarva Power gas case and Atlantic City Electric case are both "on track," but ACE has dragged since November 2024 with only a settlement expected by year-end; Pepco Maryland just filed new, with a statutory decision not due until August 2026 — a full 22-month regulatory cycle.
- **Cost discipline is explicit strategy, not incidental.** Butler: a "dedicated team on pulling cost out of our business to keep cost growth below inflation" — utilities rarely say this so directly, signaling real pressure on the O&M line to protect ROE optics.

## Investing & scarcity
- **Capital is almost entirely grid reinvestment.** 98% of net profit over five years has gone back into the system; the entire growth thesis (7.4% rate base growth through 2028) rides on transmission and reliability capex, not diversification.
- **The scarce resource is dispatchable generation supply, not capital or grid capacity.** Butler is blunt: "hoping that markets alone will fill it puts too much risk on customers" — the shortfall is explicitly framed as a supply-side, not a wires-side, problem.
- **Financing is being de-risked ahead of need.** PECO completed all planned 2025 debt issuance; ~95% of 2026 equity needs already priced through forward agreements — a defensive posture against rate/equity market volatility rather than aggressive new-money commitment.

## Where they think it's going vs what they're doing about it
- **Belief:** demand growth from AI/data centers and onshoring will structurally outrun current supply, and 2029 should show stronger EPS growth than the 2025-2028 plan as transmission investment ramps.
- **Action:** so far, action is confined to process (TSAs, cluster studies, large-load tariffs) and lobbying (advocating for utility-owned generation, engaging PJM's Critical Issue Fast Path) — none of the actual $10-15B transmission upside or new generation ownership is in the committed plan yet. The gap between "this is happening at unprecedented scale" rhetoric and "nothing enters guidance until certain" execution is the central tension of the call — Exelon is talking like an infrastructure supercycle is underway while guiding like nothing has changed.

## Hedges — what they wouldn't commit to
- **Won't quantify 2029 growth**, deferring "formal guidance on the Q4 call" despite being pressed directly on whether 2029 beats the plan's midpoint.
- **Won't commit CAMT/tax-repair cushion to a specific use** (less equity vs. more capex) — framed only as generic "momentum towards 14%" credit metrics.
- **Won't count large-load megawatts as probable** until both cluster study and signed TSA are complete, deliberately keeping 27GW of pipeline out of the "high probability" bucket despite bullish tone elsewhere.

## The street — what analysts asked
- Analysts pushed hard on the credibility of state resource-adequacy processes — Maryland's RFP shortfall versus Constellation's competing same-day proposal, and whether Pennsylvania's dueling legislative bills can actually produce a wires/IPP compromise.
- Repeated probing on whether the 47GW large-load pipeline is real or inflated, and how probability-weighting actually works — a proxy for skepticism about data-center demand claims industry-wide.
- Tax policy (CAMT/repairs) and a stalled ACE rate case rounded out the list, both process-timing questions rather than strategic ones.
- Compressed worry: **can regulated utilities convert AI-driven demand hype into bankable, de-risked capital plans faster than merchant generators and legislators can undercut them?**
