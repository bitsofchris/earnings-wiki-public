# BA — fiscal 2026-Q2 call (2026-07-28)

**The key idea:** Boeing is executing a rate-ramp story it's told for two years — 737 to 47, 787 stabilized at eight, 777X flight test accelerating — and for the first time the numbers are catching up to the narrative (highest quarterly deliveries since 2018, positive free cash flow). The tension: everything still depends on a supply chain and engine-delivery cadence Boeing doesn't fully control, and one fixed-price defense program just proved that "de-risked" doesn't mean "risk-free."

## Where they're going / what they're building
- **Rate math is the whole company strategy right now.** 737 is ramping through 47 toward 52 this summer via a new Everett production line (in addition to Renton), with 57 and eventually 63/month flagged as future rate breaks; Ortberg was explicit that "it's going to get harder as we go from 52 to 57."
- **Certifications are finally converting into delivery dates.** The FAA resumed authorizing 737 MAX and 787 airworthiness certificates this month; 737-7 testing is done and 737-10 just completed its final test flight, both clearing the way for 2027 deliveries. 777X flight testing is "more than 55%" complete toward a 2027 first delivery.
- **Wichita/Spirit integration is now a $1 billion bet.** Boeing pledged a billion dollars over several years to de-bottleneck the former Spirit facility, framed as necessary infrastructure for both higher 737 and 787 rates — not just an integration cleanup.
- **A next all-new airplane is being quietly re-architected around value capture, not just technology.** Asked directly whether innovation is the path to better industry profitability, Ortberg said Boeing is "spending a good deal of time looking at where the value is in the aircraft going forward, and how do we participate in the value chain, maybe differently than what you've classically expected us to do" — hinting at new supplier/partnership models and possibly more vertical integration, timed to when "the market is ready."
- **BDS is being told to earn its way back to relevance via backlog pricing**, not volume: management pointed to better-priced new lots on KC-46, repriced backlog, and completion of fixed-price programs as the mechanism to reach high-single-digit BDS margins "by the end of the decade."

## What's changing
- **BDS underwriting discipline is visibly different from the old Boeing.** Ortberg flagged the company is "being selective on the programs we bid" and called KC-46 EAC risk "very low" — a notable claim for the program that defined Boeing's fixed-price defense losses for years.
- **Cash flow profile is shifting from lumpy-and-hoped-for to source-of-cash.** Working capital is expected to turn into a net cash source in H2 on rising deliveries and BDS/BCA advances (including the seasonal Q4 KC-46 advance) — a structural change from the multi-year cash-burn posture.
- **787 delivery cadence is being unstuck by a Riyadh Air handoff** of previously-built aircraft that were stuck waiting on seat certification — evidence the backlog of "built but not certified" jets is starting to clear, though Ortberg cautioned more seat-cert lumpiness remains through year-end.
- **Boeing is explicitly trading near-term margin for schedule** on VC-25B (Air Force One), adding resources and moving from an FAA to a military certification basis to protect the 2028 delivery commitment — a deliberate, disclosed decision rather than a surprise slip.

## What's NOT working (or being talked around)
- **VC-25B took a $280 million reach-forward-loss charge this quarter**, explicitly tied to adding resources to protect schedule — management called it "disappointing" but framed it as the cost of keeping a firm delivery date, i.e., a program still not under control on cost even as schedule risk is being bought down.
- **GE's 787 engine deliveries have fallen behind**, and Boeing is running a joint "corrective action plan" with GE — notably, on the same day GE's public line is that there isn't an engine problem. Boeing paused Charleston production for "several days in April" to let the supply chain recover, and Rate 10 timing depends on the GE recovery landing this summer.
- **Seat certification delays on the 787 aren't over** — "I can't claim victory yet," Ortberg said, acknowledging deliveries will stay lumpy through year end even though the paperwork issue doesn't block rollout.
- **The 737/787 pricing drag is a multi-year hole, not a quarter's problem**: Jay Malave confirmed program cash margins are still "slightly above breakeven" on both programs due to legacy pricing concessions that "take some time to fully dissipate," with 2018-level 737 margins targeted only "by the end of the decade."
- **Conspicuous absence: no fresh detail on the SPEEA labor negotiation** beyond "respectful and productive" — for a company two months from a possible engineering-union work stoppage in October, management declined to characterize risk and confirmed it is separately building contingency plans "should we have a work stoppage."
- **Engine supply chain capacity is a real constraint above rate 52** — Ortberg conceded comfort at 47/52 rests partly on existing engine inventory, not just supplier throughput, and that Tier 2/3 sub-suppliers may need capital support to hit 57 and 63.
