# MCD — fiscal 2025-Q3 call (2025-11-05)

**The key idea:** McDonald's is buying back value perception it let erode — nationally re-pricing its Extra Value Meals and co-funding the discount with franchisees — betting that a temporary margin hit converts into permanent traffic gains before the corporate subsidy runs out at the end of Q1 2026. Underneath the "3.6% comp growth, record $4B restaurant margin" headline sits a structurally bifurcated U.S. consumer: high-income QSR traffic up nearly double digits, low-income down nearly double digits for two straight years, with management flatly stating that only macro relief — not anything McDonald's can do — fixes the low end.

## The read — 3-5 points from the whole transcript
1. **The EVM relaunch is a financed bet with an expiration date.** McDonald's is co-investing alongside franchisees — $40M in incremental September marketing, 50% of the EVM discount (~$15M in September ballooning to ~$75M in Q4), then a smaller net-cash-flow subsidy in Q1 2026 — after which "all of our corporate support will stop." Kempczinski's own framing reveals the risk: "by the end of Q1, our system is going to be [in] a position where it's actually going to be a better decision to continue with the EVMs than it is to go back" — i.e., success is defined as making reversal look worse than continuing, not as EVMs paying for themselves outright yet.
2. **Margin story is quietly worse than the headline.** Restaurant margin dollars crossed $4B for the first time, but Borden admitted U.S. margins actually contracted this quarter because 2.4% comp growth "wasn't enough top line growth... to offset some of the inflationary pressure" in wages, food, and paper. That's a franchise-model business telling investors its own value push is margin-dilutive in the near term.
3. **Two-year-old consumer split is calcifying, not healing.** Low-income QSR traffic down "nearly double digits," a trend "that's persisted for nearly 2 years," against high-income traffic up "nearly double digits" — and management explicitly does not expect this to change in 2026 absent macro relief ("real incomes are growing"). Kempczinski even flagged SNAP cuts as a fresh headwind on top of rent, food, and childcare inflation.
4. **Beef inflation is the specific line item everyone's watching.** "Very, very high inflation around beef prices versus what we're used to historically" is called out repeatedly as a 2026 margin risk, with food/paper inflation guided to low-to-mid-single-digits for the year despite that pressure — implying McDonald's supply chain is absorbing more of the beef spike than smaller peers.
5. **China is a pricing war, not a demand problem.** Comps positive and share gains continuing, but "there's overcapacity in China" and "a delivery war... between the 3 different delivery guys," pushing pricing down — a deflationary dynamic distinct from the U.S. affordability story, and one McDonald's frames as good for consumers, bad for the P&L.

## Economy & consumer
- **Bifurcation, not a broad slowdown.** Borden: low-income QSR visits down "high single digit," high-income up "high single digits" in Q3 — an extension, not a new trend, of nearly two years of divergence.
- **Nondiscretionary inflation is squeezing the low end specifically.** Kempczinski cites "rents... at pretty high levels," high food prices in restaurants and grocery, and expensive childcare as the drivers of low-income pullback, plus a new mention of SNAP-related pressure.
- **Breakfast remains the most macro-sensitive daypart** — "an easy daypart to either skip... or to eat... at home" — under pressure industry-wide, with McDonald's merely holding share rather than gaining it.
- **No evidence yet of fast-casual trade-down into QSR** benefiting McDonald's; management says the higher-income consumer's strength is "not a change in trend," deflecting the idea that fast-casual weakness is spilling into their traffic gains.

## The business — what's working, what's not
- **Global system-wide sales growth above 6% in constant currency for a second straight quarter**, reflecting new-unit contribution, alongside comp growth "across all segments" — breadth is real, not one region carrying the number.
- **Snack Wraps were "the most popular new chicken product launch in the U.S. in recent history"** — nearly 1 in 5 customers bought one in the launch window — but Borden conceded uptake "eased somewhat after the exceptional initial launch period," a normal decay curve being framed as sustained strength.
- **U.S. margin contraction despite record margin dollars**: the $4B system-wide milestone masks that comp growth of 2.4% in the U.S. wasn't sufficient to offset wage, food, and paper inflation locally.
- **MONOPOLY's return drove real digital engagement** — "one of the biggest digital customer acquisition events we've ever had," with 45 million 90-day active app users cited as context — a genuine funnel benefit distinct from pure promotional lift.
- **Germany and Australia are outperformance case studies**: Germany posted its "strongest comp sales results in 2 years" while extending share gains to "nearly 4 years" against industry-wide traffic declines; Australia gained share for a second straight quarter after locking in 12-month value pricing.

## Investing & scarcity
- **New category teams (chicken, beverage, beef) are the innovation engine**, explicitly designed for "increased speed of innovation and scale" — beverage is called out as a >$100B category growing faster than the broader industry.
- **Beverage test economics are being engineered for margin, not value**: Kempczinski is explicit the platform is "very much designed to drive margin... designed to drive check," distinct from the McValue/EVM value plays, while still needing competitive pricing discipline to win share.
- **China capex continues despite macro pressure** — 1,000 new restaurants this year and an upgraded Hamburger University — a long-term confidence bet layered on top of near-term deflationary/price-war conditions there.
- **The real scarcity is consumer purchasing power, not McDonald's execution or supply**: Kempczinski's answer to "what fixes the low-income consumer" is a macroeconomic wish list (real income growth, relief on cost of living) that McDonald's has no lever over — a rare admission that the company's own value programs can only blunt, not reverse, the trend.

## Where they think it's going vs what they're doing about it
- **Belief:** management expects low-income pressure to persist through 2026 and calls the current climate one where "you've just got to grind it out" (invoking Ray Kroc).
- **Action:** rather than waiting it out, they've committed real money — a stacked, time-boxed subsidy (Sept marketing + discount co-investment scaling to ~$75M in Q4, tapering into Q1 2026) — to permanently reset EVM pricing before the subsidy disappears.
- **Gap:** the belief is "the low end won't recover on its own," yet the plan explicitly ends corporate support after Q1 2026 regardless of whether the low-income consumer has actually recovered — success is being redefined as "system decides it's not worth reversing," not as the underlying consumer improving.
- **On beverages, belief and action are aligned but cautious**: they're excited about the category ("$100 billion... growing much faster than the broader industry") but deliberately kept the test to ~500 restaurants and two regions rather than scaling ahead of the data.

## Hedges — what they wouldn't commit to
- **No 2026 comp guidance.** Kempczinski, asked directly about trajectory: "I'm not going to get into trouble with giving any forecast. So I'm going to let Ian handle that one" — and Ian's answer stayed qualitative.
- **Declined to endorse the "both-ends convergence" trade-down thesis** for 2026 even when an analyst laid out a favorable scenario for them: "I'd love that scenario to play out. But I'm not going to predict whether it does."
- **No commitment to a national or international beverage rollout** — explicitly conditional on how the current two-market test performs, with international expansion "dependent on how it performs in some of those other markets."
- **Sidestepped whether U.S. EVM pricing becomes a formal permanent national-pricing model**, answering instead with intent ("we would expect it does need to continue") rather than a structural commitment.

## The street — what analysts asked
- Several questions circled the same tension: can McDonald's raise value perception AND restaurant margins simultaneously, or is the EVM investment simply margin dilution dressed up as a strategy — management's answer leaned on "more traffic eventually pays for itself" without new proof it's working yet.
- Multiple analysts probed the shelf-life of franchisee/corporate co-investment — is this a bridge to a new sustainable baseline or a subsidy addiction the system can't afford to remove — management insists it ends by Q1 2026 but the underlying consumer recovery it's betting on remains unconfirmed.
- Questions on China (macro pressure vs. reported strength elsewhere) got a candid answer about an internal delivery price war rather than a demand story, an admission not fully volunteered upfront.
- The beverage test drew multiple angles (portfolio mix, global rollout, pricing intent) that management handled by consistently under-promising ("early days," "we're not trying to make too much of an inference around what it's going to do from a comp standpoint").
- Compressed worry: **is McDonald's confusing "we bought back some low-income traffic with a subsidized discount" for "we fixed the structural problem," and what happens to comps when the subsidy checks stop clearing in Q2 2026?**
