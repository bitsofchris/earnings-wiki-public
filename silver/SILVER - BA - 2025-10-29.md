# BA — fiscal 2025-Q3 call (2025-10-29)

**The key idea:** Boeing is threading two stories at once: real operational recovery (positive free cash flow for the first time since 2023, record deliveries, FAA-approved rate increases on 737 and 787) undercut by a second consecutive 777X blowup — a $4.9B charge and a full-year delivery slip to 2027 — that management insists is a paperwork and certification-process problem, not an airplane problem. The new CFO spent the call establishing credibility by explicitly refusing to commit to numbers he hasn't yet verified.

## The read — 3-5 points from the whole transcript
1. **The recovery is real but narrow.** Positive free cash flow ($238M) for the first time in seven quarters, highest delivery quarter since 2018 (160 airplanes), and FAA-approved rate increases to 42/month on the 737 all happened in the same quarter as a $4.9B non-cash charge — the operational engine and the legacy development-program overhang are now clearly decoupled.
2. **777X failure is process, not product — and that's a double-edged claim.** Ortberg was emphatic: "There's no new issues with the airplane itself or the engines... the issue is solely around getting the certification work complete." Boeing "underestimated how much work it was going to take" to get TIA (Type Inspection Authorization) approvals from the FAA. That's more reassuring on engineering risk but more damning on program management — Boeing didn't understand its own regulatory path on a flagship jet.
3. **777X cash pain stretches to 2029.** New CFO Malave: 2026 will be "a heavy use year" similar to or worse than 2025's cash usage on the program, improving in 2027, and not reaching neutral-to-positive until 2029 — a 4-year cash tail from a single program.
4. **Rate discipline is the operating philosophy, and it's paying off.** Ortberg: "it's way more impactful for us to move when we're not ready than to hold off and wait until we're ready... I'd much rather be a month late than go a month early." The 737 KPI-gated approach to 38→42/month worked cleanly; the same 6-month-minimum cadence is now being applied to 42→47 and beyond, with supply-chain readiness (not FAA process) cited as the constraint above 47.
5. **New CFO explicitly refused to make promises.** Malave, 2.5 months into the job, declined a 2026 FCF number, declined to endorse the long-standing $10B FCF target, and deferred a full 2026 framework to January — a credibility play after a CFO transition, but it also means Wall Street left the call with no real 2026 anchor.

## Economy & consumer
- **No macro read offered.** Boeing frames demand purely through its own backlog and order book, not broader economic conditions — this call is a company-specific recovery story, not an economic-conditions data point.
- **Backlog as the demand proxy.** Total company backlog exceeds $600B, BCA backlog alone is $535B with more than 5,900 airplanes, and both 737 and 787 are "sold firm into the next decade" — management repeatedly leans on backlog depth rather than incoming order momentum to signal demand health (161 net orders in the quarter is modest against that backlog).
- **Consumer section omitted** — Boeing's customers are airlines and militaries; the aftermarket/services segment (BGS, +10% revenue, 17.5% operating margin, book-to-bill of 1.2) is the best available signal of airline-level traffic and fleet-utilization health, and it was uniformly strong.

## The business — what's working, what's not
- **Quality metrics improving materially.** "75% reduction in traveled work on our 737 and a reduction of 60% across all airplane programs" — a genuine operational signal following years of FAA scrutiny post-MAX and post-door-plug incidents.
- **FAA delegation restored, partially.** The FAA will now allow Boeing to self-issue airworthiness certificates for some 737 MAX and 787 units — a meaningful trust signal after the post-2024 clampdown, though Ortberg called it "a responsibility we take very seriously," aware of how fragile that trust still is.
- **BCA operating margin cratered to -48.3%** entirely on the 777X charge — revenue was actually up nearly 50% to $11.1B on higher deliveries, so the underlying delivery business is healthy; the charge is masking real progress in the reported numbers.
- **Defense (BDS) stabilizing but still fragile.** Operating margin improved to a still-thin 1.7%, with "minor cost updates on a few programs" including the KC-46 tanker absorbing shared costs from the 777X reset — cross-program contamination from the 777X problem is spreading into defense accounting.
- **St. Louis IAM strike ongoing** with production of JDAMs continuing at pre-strike rates via contingency staffing — management called the financial impact "immaterial" this quarter but didn't address a resolution timeline.

## Investing & scarcity
- **Charleston 787 site is doubling its footprint**, explicitly to support rates "in the teens" by around 2028 — Ortberg confirmed this capital is being deployed ahead of near-term need ("if we thought capping at 10 was as far as we go, we would not be investing"), a clear bet on sustained widebody demand.
- **CapEx stepping up in 2026** for both Charleston and St. Louis expansion, though Malave declined to size it, deferring to the January guide.
- **The binding constraint is regulatory throughput, not capital or demand.** On 777X: certification analysis and FAA data-submission review, not money or engineering, is what's actually gating the timeline. On 737-7/-10: same story — engine anti-ice design is "the critical path," resolved via 3,000+ hours of lab testing but still awaiting FAA certification of the design changes.
- **787 seat certification is a specific, named bottleneck**: "seats will continue to be a constraining item for us" as 787 moves from 8 to 10/month — a supplier-certification issue Boeing has flagged on multiple prior calls without full resolution.

## Where they think it's going vs what they're doing about it
- **They believe 737 can sustain a rate cadence of one step every 6 months** (42→47→52...) and are already loading the factory at 42 ahead of full rollout — actions (pretesting, staged loading) match the stated belief here, a rare case of full alignment.
- **They believe 777X is now on a "higher confidence" plan** after the reset, and back it with the $4.9B charge that explicitly provisions for rework, learning-curve costs, and extended carrying costs — the charge itself is the tangible commitment behind the belief, though this is the second major reset on this program, which limits how much credibility the "higher confidence" framing can claim.
- **They believe Charleston will need capacity for rates "in the teens"** and are funding a footprint doubling now for a 2028 payoff — capital committed years ahead of the need, a genuine forward bet rather than reactive spending.
- **Gap: cash-positive talk vs. no committed 2026 number.** Leadership repeatedly signals optimism ("things are trending favorably, and we're bullish on our outlook") without putting a figure behind 2026 free cash flow — the belief is stated, the commitment is withheld.

## Hedges — what they wouldn't commit to
- **New CFO declined to endorse the $10B FCF target.** Asked directly whether the long-standing $10B free-cash-flow target is one he's "willing to endorse," Malave gave a general confidence statement about "underlying cash generation capability" but explicitly would not attach a number or timeline, deferring a framework to a later date.
- **No 2026 free-cash-flow guidance at all** — Malave: "it's early for me to really make a strong kind of call on that right now... I'll give you just a lot more color on that in January."
- **No CapEx figure for 2026** despite confirming CapEx will rise for Charleston and St. Louis — sizing deferred to January.
- **No firm month for 787's 8→10/month move** — "I'm not going to put a month on that yet."

- **Rate-increase mechanics and FAA process parity** (737 to 47/52, 787 to 10+) were probed in detail, testing whether the smoother 38→42 approval process will hold at higher rates — Ortberg was confident on process but hedged on timing above 47.
- **2026 cash flow and the $10B target** came up from multiple angles (Deutsche Bank, Jefferies) and got the same deferral each time — the new CFO's unwillingness to commit to a number was itself a recurring theme analysts kept probing.
- **M&A and capital allocation** (Jeppesen sale, Spirit reintegration) drew a narrow answer — no appetite signaled for anything beyond those two already-announced transactions.
