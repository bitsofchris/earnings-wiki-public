# VRSK — fiscal 2025-Q4 call (2026-02-18)

**The key idea:** Verisk killed its biggest inorganic growth bet (AccuLinks, terminated after a dragged-out FTC review) and pivoted hard to shareholder returns ($1.5B ASR, dividend hike) while betting its organic growth defense rests on AI making its data more valuable rather than more replaceable. Nearly every analyst question, in one form or another, asked the same underlying thing: does AI strengthen Verisk's data moat, or does it let clients and competitors route around it?

## The read — 3-5 points from the whole transcript
1. **A failed acquisition became a capital-return story.** Verisk walked from AccuLinks after the FTC extended its review — "the opportunity cost of waiting on the sidelines... was too high" — redeemed the associated $1.5B in debt, then turned around and announced a $1.5B accelerated buyback plus an 11% dividend increase, alongside divesting Verisk Marketing Solutions to sharpen the pure-play insurance-data focus.
2. **AI is framed as a moat reinforcement, not a threat, but the margin guide doesn't fully back that up.** Management repeatedly argued that clean, contributory, industry-wide data is *more* valuable in an AI world because AI needs trusted inputs — yet 2026 guidance shows margins expanding only modestly off a normalized 55.8% base to 56%-56.5%, with an analyst directly challenging whether any underlying (non-divestiture) leverage exists at all.
3. **Weather and auto softness are being treated as temporary, not structural.** Transactional revenue fell 6.5% in Q4, primarily comping against 2024's Hurricane Helene/Milton activity and low 2025 weather; management calls this a trough dynamic that resolves through 2026, though personal-lines auto competitive pressure gets a separate, less time-bound explanation.
4. **Client AI adoption is bifurcated by size.** Large, sophisticated carriers want Verisk's raw data to build their own AI; smaller/midsized clients prefer Verisk-built AI products for faster ROI — both paths keep Verisk's data at the center, according to Shavel, but this is Verisk's own framing of a dynamic that could also be read as large clients disintermediating them over time.
5. **Pricing power is softening slightly but still historically strong.** CFO Mann: after three years of a "historically very strong pricing environment," 2026 pricing "may be modestly coming down versus the prior year" — a rare, understated admission that the multi-year price-increase tailwind is decelerating.

## Economy & consumer
- **Insurance industry health as the demand backdrop.** Shavel: 2025 saw "solid mid-single-digit net written premium growth" and "consistently better year-over-year combined ratios," which management frames as fueling carrier appetite to spend on data/analytics/AI tools — no direct end-consumer commentary since Verisk sells to insurers, not consumers.
- **Hard-to-soft market transition seen as a net positive for Verisk.** Asked directly about the P&C pricing cycle, Shavel called the shift "cautiously" an "improving sales cycle," reasoning that lower carrier growth pushes carriers toward more tools to find profitable growth — a self-serving but plausible read of the cycle.

## The business — what's working, what's not
- **Subscription strength is the core positive.** Subscription revenue (84% of total) grew 7.7% OCC in Q4, "compounding from the 11%... increase... in 2024," led by forms/rules/loss costs, catastrophe and risk solutions (renamed from Extreme Event Solutions), and antifraud.
- **Transactional and auto are the weak spots.** Transactional revenue (16% of total) fell 6.5% OCC, driven by low weather activity hitting Property Estimating Solutions and continued softness in personal-lines auto, where one-time nonrate-action product revenue "peaked in 2024 and is minimal now" and undifferentiated products face direct competitive pressure.
- **Core Lines Reimagine program nearing completion.** 22 customer-facing modules shipped in 2025 (ahead of a 20-module target), with 25 more planned in 2026 to complete "the original scope of the Reimagine investment program" — after which the company pivots to AI-driven continuous innovation on top of that platform.
- **A government contract work stoppage will drag Q1 2026** specifically, layered on top of the weather- and comp-driven softness already flagged.

## Investing & scarcity
- **Capital redirected from M&A to buybacks/dividends.** Post-AccuLinks-termination, Verisk raised repurchase authorization to $2.5B, launching an immediate $1.5B ASR and an 11% dividend increase to $2/share annually — a clear signal that management sees no comparably attractive near-term deployment for the freed-up balance sheet capacity.
- **Capex guided modestly up ($260M-$280M) as "highest return on capital opportunity,"** prioritizing organic investment over acquisitions after the AccuLinks experience.
- **The real constraint is client absorption capacity, not Verisk's own supply.** Repeated references to "dedicating a lot of time to training" clients on new modules and needing "training thousands of employees" (per a partner-firm CEO) suggest adoption friction sits on the client side, not Verisk's build capacity — over 35 AI-powered projects are already live.

## Where they think it's going vs what they're doing about it
- **Belief: AI increases the value of Verisk's data; Action: hiring for it, not yet pricing for it.** Shavel is confident AI reinforces the moat and hired Steve Cotterer specifically to lead claims-AI transformation — but the 2026 guide shows only modest margin expansion and management wouldn't commit to specific price-increase figures, leaving a gap between stated conviction and priced-in financial commitment.
- **Belief: transactional revenue recovers through 2026; Action: no specific lever named beyond comp normalization.** When pressed on the "path to recovery," CFO Mann's answer leaned on comp math (tough 2024 storm comps rolling off) rather than a described operational initiative — the recovery thesis is largely mean-reversion, not a new product driving growth.
- **Belief: clients want AI-ready data delivery; Action: building MCP servers.** Shavel explicitly cites investment in "model context protocols and MCP servers" as the concrete technical response to large-client demand for AI-consumable data — a specific, verifiable infrastructure commitment (notably, direct Anthropic-ecosystem-adjacent tooling for data delivery).

## Hedges — what they wouldn't commit to
- **No specific pricing figures.** Mann declined to give "specific annual price ranges," offering only a qualitative "modestly coming down... but still historically very strong" characterization when directly asked.
- **No quarter-by-quarter growth guide.** Repeatedly declined to translate the "Q1 revenue down low-single-digits sequentially" guidance into a specific organic constant-currency year-over-year number, despite two separate analysts pushing for it.
- **Dodged a direct margin-math challenge.** When an analyst laid out math suggesting all 2026 margin expansion traces to the VMS divestiture, management didn't confirm or refute the specific numbers, instead saying "we can take that offline with you" — a notable non-answer on a substantive, embarrassing-if-true claim.

## The street — what analysts asked
- **The dominant theme was AI moat durability**, asked from at least four angles: whether clients prefer self-built AI on Verisk data versus buying Verisk AI products, whether software is "tied" to or separable from the underlying data, whether Vibe coding threatens vertical software broadly, and whether sophisticated clients will end up using more or less Verisk data over time.
- **A secondary cluster probed margin credibility**, with one analyst directly challenging whether any 2026 margin expansion exists outside the VMS divestiture — a pointed question management sidestepped rather than answered on the call.
- **Transactional recovery timing and auto competitive dynamics** got specific, standalone questions, reflecting investor concern that these are real (not just weather-comp) drags.
- **Compressed worry:** is Verisk's data genuinely becoming more valuable as an AI input, or is the company's software/analytics layer at risk of being commoditized while its data gets extracted by better-resourced AI-native competitors and sophisticated clients building in-house?
