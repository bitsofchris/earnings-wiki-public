# BA — fiscal 2025-Q4 call (2026-01-27)

**The key idea:** Boeing posted its best quarterly revenue since 2018 and a record backlog across all three segments, and management is confident enough to reiterate a $10B free cash flow target for the medium term — but the quarter also delivered a fresh $565M charge on the tanker program, an unresolved 777X engine durability issue, and a CFO who wouldn't quantify roughly $6-7B of "temporary" cash headwinds baked into 2026 guidance. This is a company visibly stabilizing operationally (rate discipline, rework reduction, on-time delivery up threefold on the MAX) while still absorbing the financial scar tissue of years of program mismanagement, now compounded by a fresh, still-integrating Spirit AeroSystems acquisition.

## The read — 3-5 points from the whole transcript
1. **The turnaround is real but incomplete, by management's own framing.** Ortberg opened by explicitly cautioning "we haven't fully turned the corner" despite delivering 600 airplanes (most since 2018) and winning 1,173 net orders. The tension between record top-line/backlog numbers and still-negative segment margins (-5.6% BCA, -6.8% BDS) is the story of the quarter.
2. **The tanker charge undercuts the "programs are stabilizing" narrative.** BDS took a $565M KC-46A charge tied to higher Everett production-support costs and Spirit-related supply chain costs — Ortberg called it "disappointing" but framed it as a deliberate investment (overstaffing quality/engineering) to hit 19 tanker deliveries in 2026 versus 14 in 2025.
3. **Spirit AeroSystems is now Boeing's problem to fix, and the market remembers 2018.** When directly asked whether Spirit will repeat its historical struggles scaling to rate 52, Ortberg acknowledged "Spirit has some work to do" and framed the acquisition itself as the risk mitigant — "if they had continued in a distressed environment, I think that risk would have been significantly higher than our ability to go manage that."
4. **The $10B free cash flow target survives an unusually candid list of unresolved headwinds.** CFO Jay Mollave itemized 777X cash burn (turning positive only in 2029), 737/787 "customer considerations" and "excess advances" from prior delivery delays, and residual BDS charge runoff — then declined to quantify any of them individually beyond an aggregate "$6-7 billion," repeatedly telling analysts he'd give "more color in the future."
5. **A blunt structural question went unanswered.** Asked point-blank whether commercial aircraft manufacturing can ever be a genuinely profitable business given how much value accrues to suppliers, engine makers, and the aftermarket versus the OEM, Ortberg pivoted to "how we manage risk in contracts" and a next-airplane strategy discussion rather than addressing the current duopoly economics — a notable non-answer on the core profitability question of the business.

## Economy & consumer
- **No macro read given** — this is a duopoly aerospace OEM selling into an airline/government customer base with multi-year backlogs, insulating the call from near-term consumer-spending commentary.
- **Trade policy is the closest proxy for "economy" risk cited.** Ortberg described the tariff environment as "super dynamic" but said the administration has been "accessible" and "supportive," with China deliveries running at the same pace as last year after a prior shutdown was resolved.
- **Demand signals are unambiguously strong at the order level**: Alaska Airlines' largest-ever order, 1,173 net commercial orders for the year, and record 787 order intake (395 in 2025) — but order strength doesn't map directly into current cash generation given delivery delays.

## The business — what's working, what's not
- **What's working: production discipline and quality metrics.** 737 on-time delivery improved threefold year over year, 787 rework hours fell nearly 30%, and 5,100+ work instructions were simplified — concrete operational proof points, not just rhetoric.
- **What's not: segment profitability remains negative.** BCA operating margin was -5.6% and BDS was -6.8%, both improved year over year but still loss-making, with the quarter's headline EPS ($9.92) almost entirely an artifact of the Digital Aviation Solutions divestiture gain ($11.83/share) rather than operating earnings.
- **BGS is the one clean bright spot.** Adjusted for the divestiture gain, BGS grew revenue 6% with 18.6% margins and record backlog — a stable, high-margin services business subsidizing a still-recovering manufacturing core.
- **The tanker charge is a reminder that fixed-price defense legacy risk hasn't fully burned off**, even as Ortberg insists it's isolated and "doesn't really reflect at all on any of the other BDS programs."

## Investing & scarcity
- **CapEx is stepping up materially**: ~$3B in 2025 to ~$4B in 2026, funding the Everett North Line (737 capacity above 47/month, "facility and tooling investments are now complete"), Charleston 787 expansion, and near-complete F-47/PAC-3 defense capacity.
- **The binding constraint is supply chain readiness for the next rate breaks, not demand or capital.** Ortberg was explicit that 42→47 on the 737 is manageable via existing inventory, but 47→52 will require genuine "supply chain harmony" — the harder ramp is still ahead, not behind.
- **Spirit acquisition is itself a scarcity/control play** — buying the supplier to directly manage capacity investment and quality risk rather than relying on a financially distressed third party during the highest-stakes rate ramp in years.
- **787 delivery constraint is now seat certification, not production** — "still dealing with seat issues... that's less of a constraint to our production output, but more of an issue with deliveries," expected to persist.
