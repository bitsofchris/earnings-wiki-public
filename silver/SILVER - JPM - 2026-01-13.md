# JPM — fiscal 2025-Q4 call (2026-01-13)

**The key idea:** JPMorgan posted a record year (18% ROTCE, $57.5B full-year net income ex-items) and is leaning further into investment — a ~$9B expense increase for 2026, the two-year Apple Card integration, continued AWM hiring — while explicitly refusing to defend the spend line-by-line ("trust but verify... you gotta just trust me," Dimon told a skeptical analyst). The tension underneath: management's own framing ("NII is an outcome," "efficiency ratio is an output") pushes back hard against being modeled as a bank chasing returns, even as the quarter closed with a $2.2B reserve build tied to the Apple Card portfolio and a still-unresolved political threat (proposed card APR caps, stablecoin interest loopholes) hanging over the card business that's funding much of this growth.

## The read — 3-5 points from the whole transcript
1. **Political risk arrived overnight and management has no playbook yet.** A presidential social-media post floated credit-card APR caps; Barnum wouldn't even speculate on mechanics: "this is happening very quickly in a sort of unconventional way starting with a social media post... I just don't have anything for you." He warned price controls wouldn't compress margins but would cut off credit access "especially the people who need it the most, chronically."
2. **The Apple Card deal is real but costly to integrate — two years, not because of red tape but because Apple built a genuinely separate tech stack.** "They actually built a completely different integrated into iOS tech stack... we have to rebuild what their tech stack is, embedded into our system," Barnum said, calling the deal "economically compelling" and "a win-win-win."
3. **Dimon explicitly rejects the return-on-equity framing analysts use to model the bank.** "NII is just an outcome... on itself, almost irrelevant" and efficiency ratio is similarly "an output" of capital deployment decisions, not a target — a deliberate reframing after Mike Mayo pressed on the $9B expense jump.
4. **Consumer deposit growth keeps getting pushed out** as yield-seeking flows into investment products (client assets +17%) outlast expectations, even as checking-account growth (1.7M net new accounts) stays robust — a gap between franchise growth and balance growth that's now run multiple quarters.
5. **Non-bank financial institution (NBFI) lending exposure has grown sharply over seven years** — Barnum devoted extended remarks to defending the $160B book, noting "we've only seen one charge-off" since 2018 (fraud-related) and framing the growth as a rational response to bank capital rules and leverage-lending guidelines, not a hidden risk.

## Economy & consumer
- **Dimon's near-term read is constructive but caveated hard on the long tail.** "In the short run, call it six months and nine months and even a year... that's pretty positive," citing consumer cash, stimulus from the "big beautiful bill," and deregulation — but flagged geopolitical risk and unsustainable deficits: "it will bite eventually."
- **No sign of consumer deterioration despite weak sentiment surveys.** "Despite weak consumer sentiment, trends in our data are largely consistent with historical norms and we are not currently seeing deterioration," with debit/credit sales volume up 7% y/y.
- **Deposit-per-account inflection keeps slipping.** Yield-seeking flows are "much, much lower than they were at the peak but are still not exactly zero," delaying the expected return to consumer deposit growth into 2026, below the 6% Investor Day scenario.
- **A Fed liquidity tailwind is emerging that wasn't in original guidance.** Dimon flagged the Fed's ~$40B/month T-bill purchases adding to bank reserves, mostly landing first in high-beta wholesale deposits.

## The business — what's working, what's not
- **Record results across wealth/asset management:** AWM pretax margin 38%, revenue up 13% y/y, record $553B full-year client asset net inflows, "positive across all channels, regions, and asset classes."
- **Equities markets revenue up 40%** on "robust performance across the franchise, particularly in prime," while fixed income's gain was tempered by weaker credit trading.
- **IB fees fell y/y** — attributed to a strong prior-year comp and deals "pushed to 2026," with management "quite optimistic" the pipeline reasserts.
- **Card normalization is starting to show up as credit cost, not stress:** 2026 charge-off rate guided to ~3.4%; wholesale provisions ticked slightly negative (downgrades > upgrades) with a loss-given-default parameter update, which Barnum called "not particularly concerning" and largely a normalization off exceptionally low historical charge-off levels.

## Investing & scarcity
- **~$9B expense increase for 2026 (to ~$105B)** funds rural/international branch expansion, payment systems, personalization, and AI — Dimon frames it as unapologetic growth investment: "if you really believe that they're real, you should be doing that. That's the right way to grow a company."
- **Headcount and real estate catch-up:** after years of hot-desking/remote-work-driven underinvestment, the firm is now dedensifying and renovating space across all 320,000 employees as an "in-office company" — a small but notable reallocation.
- **Capital is abundant, not the binding constraint:** Dimon says JPM would run with "$30-40 billion or more" of excess capital under any plausible final rule, and argues the system should focus less on capital and more on liquidity/interest-rate exposure/resolvability.
- **The real scarce resource is integration bandwidth and competitive positioning**, not capital or compute — the two-year Apple Card buildout, and an explicit read of the competitive field naming Stripe, SoFi, Revolut, and Schwab as who they benchmark against for tech investment.

## Where they think it's going vs what they're doing about it
- **Says:** "moderately optimistic" outlook for wholesale C&I loan growth and a recovering IB fee environment in 2026. **Does:** guides card loan growth down to 6-7% (revolver-normalization tailwind ending) and total NII ex-markets roughly flat at ~$95B — the stated optimism is narrower and more hedged than the tone suggests.
- **Says:** AI "will be driving more efficiency down the road" and spend "will be spending more." **Does:** refuses to quantify AI/tech spend at all, citing competitive sensitivity — a gap between the confident forward claim and the deliberately opaque commitment.
- **Says:** capital rules should be "done correctly," not goal-seeked to a target. **Does:** already carries what Dimon calls maximal excess capital regardless of where rules land, an implicit hedge against regulatory uncertainty even while arguing against the need for buffers in principle.

## Hedges — what they wouldn't commit to
- **No guidance on fee revenue or NIR**, ever, "given how market dependent they are and volatile they are."
- **Declined to speculate on APR-cap mechanics or timeline**, or on stablecoin interest-loophole legislation outcomes, citing how fast and informally (a "social media post") these developments emerged.
- **Refused to break out AI/tech spend by dollar figure**, Dimon: "we're not going to give you information which puts us at a competitive disadvantage."
- **Declined to state a target buffer above regulatory minimums**: "There should be no buffers... these capital numbers are already set to handle maximum stress."

## The street — what analysts asked
- **Political/regulatory tail risk dominated:** stablecoin interest loopholes, credit-card APR caps via presidential directive, capital-rule trajectory (GSIB/Basel), and DOJ subpoenas of the Fed chair — a cluster reflecting anxiety that the deregulation tailwind investors priced in for 2026 is now offset by executive-overreach risk.
- **Persistent skepticism on the $9B expense jump:** one analyst pressed hard for line-item justification and was rebuffed with an appeal to trust rather than data — management gave thematic color (tech, branches, real estate catch-up, healthcare inflation) but declined a breakdown.
- **Credit and deposit-competition questions were comparatively benign,** with management repeatedly characterizing both as "nothing that concerning" and "always very competitive."
- **Compressed worry:** is JPM's growth investment cycle a rational bet on a structurally advantaged franchise, or is management using "trust me" to avoid explaining why expenses are outrunning revenue in a year where political risk to the card business just spiked overnight?
