# PLTR — fiscal 2025-Q4 call (2026-02-02)

**The key idea:** Palantir posted its best growth quarter as a public company (70% revenue growth, Rule of 127) and is guiding to 61% growth for 2026 — a number CEO Alex Karp repeatedly calls historically unprecedented for enterprise software. The tension is that nearly all of this is concentrated in the U.S. (commercial +137%, government +66%) while international commercial grew just 8% YoY and management explicitly frames that gap as a political/institutional failure abroad rather than something Palantir intends to fix.

## The read — 3-5 points from the whole transcript
1. **U.S.-only hypergrowth, international stagnation.** International commercial revenue grew only 8% YoY to $171M versus 137% YoY domestic commercial — and Karp's explanation is institutional, not competitive: European buyers "want to buy products from themselves" and lack the "load bearing" purchasing structures to choose the best product over the domestic one.
2. **Net dollar retention (139%) still understates the growth story.** CFO Dave Glazer notes NDR "does not include revenue from new customers acquired in the past 12 months," so it "does not yet fully capture the acceleration in velocity" — a metric caveat worth remembering when comparing quarters.
3. **Land-and-expand cycle times have collapsed to weeks.** A healthcare company signed a $96M deal after two boot camps; an engineering services firm signed $80M after a fall demo season. Existing accounts are compounding fast too — one utility went from $7M to $31M ACV within a year.
4. **Deliberate refusal of "slot" software and M&A.** Karp: "The reality of Palantir is we're not a slot company," and the company explicitly won't do acquisitions, framing it as cultural fit risk — an unusual stance for a company at this growth rate and cash position ($7.2B cash).
5. **Reindustrialization is expanding beyond shipbuilding.** ShipOS (submarines) is described as the template Shyam Sankar expects to extend to "fighters, bombers, surface vessels, drones, weapons themselves, munitions" — a real expansion of defense TAM if it materializes, but still anecdote-stage.

## Economy & consumer
- No traditional consumer discussed — Palantir's customers are enterprises and governments. The macro lens instead is a claimed **AI-adoption bifurcation**: "AI haves are defining the future of their industries, while... AI have nots are fighting for survival in the present" (Ryan Taylor).
- Karp extends this to a geopolitical claim: adoption is concentrated in the **U.S. and China**, with the Gulf states and Israel following, and **Northern Europe, Canada, and Continental Europe lagging** — attributed to institutional/political rigidity rather than technology gaps.

## The business — what's working, what's not
- **What's working:** Record TCV bookings of $4.3B (+138% YoY), 61 deals over $10M closed in the quarter, and U.S. business crossing $1B in quarterly revenue for the first time.
- **What's not/what's structurally narrow:** International commercial (8% YoY) and to a lesser extent international government (43% YoY off a small base, largely U.K.-driven) are the visible soft spots inside an otherwise blowout quarter. Strategic commercial contracts (the legacy SPAC-era equity deals) are being wound down to under $7M for all of 2026 — a clean-up of a previously criticized revenue-quality issue, worth noting since it removes a prior source of skepticism about "real" revenue.
- Customer count grew only 34% YoY versus 70% revenue growth — Karp calls this gap "inexplicable" and treats it as proof of deepening wallet share rather than address a concentration risk directly.

## Investing & scarcity
- Adjusted expenses up 34% YoY, explicitly tied to **AIP investment and "elite technical hiring"** — headcount/talent, not compute, is the scarcity management names directly.
- The stated constraint is **operational bandwidth for complexity outside the U.S.**: "we really don't have the bandwidth to do anything that's difficult outside of America... the demand in the U.S. is so great." This is a company choosing depth over breadth by design, not by capital limitation — cash balance is $7.2B and growing.

## Where they think it's going vs what they're doing about it
- **Belief:** management insists the current growth rate is not tethered to a "broader category" — it's company-specific ("N-of-1") — and guides 61% growth for 2026 regardless of broader AI market sentiment.
- **Action backing it up:** concrete commitments include the 8-week submarine industrial base tech fellowship, Maven rollout to "all combatant commands" this fiscal year, and continued elite technical hiring funding the 34% YoY expense growth.
- **Gap:** on international reacceleration, belief (Europe "has the clearest idea of the problem," per Karp on France) is not matched by action — no international investment, headcount, or go-to-market commitment was described; the company is content to wait for foreign institutions to change rather than build toward them.

## Hedges — what they wouldn't commit to
- No commitment to accelerating or investing in international expansion — Karp reframes it as the counterparties' problem: "the issue ends up being their issue more than our issue."
- No acquisition activity, framed as principled rather than opportunistic, effectively taking inorganic growth off the table as a lever regardless of market conditions.
- Declined to give customer-count-level detail behind the "inexplicable" revenue-per-customer growth, leaving the concentration question qualitative.

## The street — what analysts asked
- Questions clustered around two anxieties: (1) whether 2026 AI commercial demand is real and broadening across the customer base, versus concentrated hype ("the show-me story"), and (2) whether Palantir's footprint within accounts — commercial and defense — is expanding beyond original contract scope, i.e., is Palantir capturing disproportionate wallet share once inside an account.
- Both questions were answered with anecdotes (specific deal sizes, specific customer quotes) rather than aggregate account-level data, and management leaned into unfalsifiable claims ("it will work and it will work really well") rather than disaggregating growth into price, volume, and new-logo components.
- Compressed worry: **is Palantir's growth broad-based adoption, or a small number of very deep, very concentrated relationships that could plateau if even one or two anchor accounts slow down?**
