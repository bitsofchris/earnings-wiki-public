# MSFT — fiscal 2026-Q3 call (2026-04-29)

**The key idea:** Microsoft is mid-transition from selling software seats to selling metered agent usage, and the entire call is management justifying a near-$190B calendar-year CapEx commitment as rational pursuit of demand that already outstrips supply. The tension: bookings and near-term revenue metrics are getting noisier and harder to read precisely because the business model itself is changing shape underneath them, and management is asking investors to trust the "book of business" (>$600B RPO) over any single quarter's bookings print.

## The read — 3-5 points from the whole transcript
1. **Capacity, not demand, is the ceiling.** Azure grew 40% against demand that Hood says "continues to exceed available capacity" — the constraint is physical (GPUs, CPUs, storage, memory pricing), and management states outright they expect to "remain constrained at least through 2026."
2. **The pricing model is being rebuilt in real time.** GitHub Copilot flipped to usage-based pricing effective June 1; Satya frames the whole portfolio — productivity, coding, security — as converging on "per-user and usage" hybrid pricing, which Amy Hood says will make traditional bookings a noisier signal going forward.
3. **Copilot crossed from adoption to habit.** M365 Copilot weekly engagement is now "at the same level as Outlook," seat adds hit +250% y/y (fastest since launch), and monthly active usage of first-party agents is up 6x year-to-date — this is the one metric line where the story clearly outran expectations.
4. **The OpenAI relationship was renegotiated toward Microsoft's IP leverage.** Frontier model IP is now royalty-free through 2032 with the OpenAI revenue-share to Microsoft eliminated, while OpenAI itself remains a large compute customer — Hood calls the revenue-share visibility through 2030 "a real positive."
5. **Consumer is in retreat/rebuild mode while commercial accelerates.** Windows OEM guided down mid-to-high teens, Xbox down low teens, against commercial cloud and AI accelerating — management is explicit this is a deliberate "win back fans" pivot to fundamentals rather than growth.

## Economy & consumer
- **No broad macro read offered, deflected to business-outcome funding.** When an analyst asked directly where AI spending dollars come from given flat CIO IT-budget surveys and flat GDP growth, Satya didn't point to macro tailwinds — he said budgets get reshaped by "business outcomes making their way into IT budgets, and maybe reallocation from other line items."
- **Consumer businesses (Windows, Xbox) are explicitly playing defense.** Satya: "we are focused on fundamentals, prioritizing quality and serving our core users better" — Game Pass price changes and Windows performance work framed as responses to complaints, not growth bets.
- **Memory/component pricing is now hitting consumer hardware demand directly.** Windows OEM decline attributed partly to "a lower PC market as prices increase due to memory cost" — a real-economy input-cost signal bleeding into a tech giant's guidance.

## The business — what's working, what's not
- **Cloud and AI are the engine: $54.5B Microsoft Cloud revenue (+29%), AI ARR over $37B (+123%).** Azure specifically grew 40% in constant currency against what Hood calls "a strong prior year comparable that included accelerating growth" — a genuinely hard comp to beat and it still accelerated.
- **M365 Copilot's seat base ramp is unusually steep**: over 20 million paid seats, customers with 50,000+ seats quadrupled y/y, and Accenture alone signed 740,000 seats — "our largest Copilot win to date."
- **On-prem server and Windows OEM/devices are structurally declining** — on-prem down mid-single digits guided, consistent with ongoing cloud migration, not new information but confirms no reversal.
- **Headcount is shrinking even as the AI business scales**, funded partly by a ~$900M one-time voluntary retirement program — a deliberate efficiency lever run alongside record capacity investment, worth flagging for anyone tracking AI-driven labor substitution inside a hyperscaler itself.

## Investing & scarcity
- **CapEx guide: over $40B in Q4, ~$190B for calendar 2026** — a sequential and year-over-year step-up that one analyst characterized as requiring a ~$120B pickup in H2 versus H1, largely to chase demand rather than build ahead of it.
- **~2/3 of CapEx is short-lived assets (GPUs/CPUs)** — directly tied to near-term revenue capture, not speculative long-horizon building, which Hood repeatedly uses to defend the spend against margin-dilution concerns.
- **The binding constraint is stated plainly**: "Even with these additional investments and continued efforts to bring GPU, CPU, and storage capacity online faster, we expect to remain constrained at least through 2026." This is as close to an admission of hard supply-chain limits as a hyperscaler CFO makes on a call.
- **First-party silicon (Maia 200, Cobalt) is being pushed as a margin lever**, not just a performance one — Maia 200 offers "over 30% improved tokens per dollar," and Cobalt is being expanded specifically because large customers are choosing it as they scale.

## Where they think it's going vs what they're doing about it
- **Belief: usage-based pricing will define the next phase of every per-seat business.** Action: GitHub Copilot converted to usage-based pricing (effective June 1) — the one product line where belief and action are fully aligned already.
- **Belief: Azure growth accelerates in H2 calendar 2026.** Action: the CapEx guide backs this — a genuine money-behind-the-belief commitment, though it also means if H2 acceleration doesn't materialize, the CapEx overhang becomes a much harder story to defend.
- **Belief: agentic AI is a "structural position" in knowledge work, coding, and security TAM.** Action: acquisition of model diversity (Foundry supporting OpenAI, Anthropic, open source) and first-party MAI models (Transcribe-1, Image-2) to reduce COGS dependency on any single model provider — a hedging move against believing any one partner's models will remain best-in-class.
- **Gap worth flagging:** management is confident enough to guide double-digit revenue/opex growth for FY2027 while simultaneously saying they cannot predict how bookings will behave as the seat/usage mix shifts — they're committing capital ahead of having a clean read on the metric (bookings) that traditionally validates that kind of commitment.

## Hedges — what they wouldn't commit to
- **No specific consumption-vs-seat revenue mix, even on a 3-5 year horizon** — asked directly, Satya described the mechanics (bundled base usage plus overage) but declined to size the eventual split.
- **No near-term bookings guidance tied to the pricing transition** — Hood: "you may not see it in the short term in bookings," effectively asking investors to look past a metric that's about to get structurally noisier without offering a replacement number to anchor on yet.
- **CapEx allocation between first-party and third-party (Azure customer) use is described only qualitatively** ("we continue to balance the incoming supply we can allocate here against our other high ROI priorities") — no hard split disclosed despite a direct analyst request for a framework.

## The street — what analysts asked
- **Recurring anxiety: paying for it.** Multiple questions circled the same worry from different angles — Keith Weiss opened with "who's paying for all of this" against flat CIO budget surveys and GDP; Karl Keirstead and Mark Moerdler both pressed on whether CapEx growth is outrunning revenue growth and whether the physical supply chain can actually absorb $190B.
- **Margin skepticism, directly named.** Brent Thill named the "AI is gonna be really expensive" pushback outright and asked what investors are missing — Hood's answer (better-than-cloud-cycle margins, free IP from OpenAI, first-party silicon, efficiency work) is doing a lot of work to counter a thesis analysts are clearly still holding.
- **OpenAI deal mechanics** drew a direct question on what changed financially — answered concretely (royalty-free IP to 2032, revenue share to Microsoft eliminated, revenue share to OpenAI predictable through 2030) rather than dodged.
- **No pushback on Copilot numbers themselves** — the seat-growth and usage-intensity metrics were treated as credible and impressive across the board; skepticism concentrated entirely on the capital and margin side of the story, not the product-adoption side.
- **Compressed worry:** can Microsoft turn a $190B capacity bet into revenue fast enough, and will the shift to usage-based pricing make that turn visible in the numbers before investor patience runs out.
