# FAST — fiscal 2025-Q3 call (2025-10-13)

**The key idea:** Fastenal delivered its strongest quarterly growth since 2023 (11.7%) entirely through share gains and internal execution — the fastener restocking initiative and deepening large-account penetration — while the broader industrial economy stayed in outright contraction (PMI 48.6). The tension underneath: pricing actions meant to offset tariffs are running behind plan, not because customers are pushing back, but because management is deliberately slow-walking price to preserve trust and find substitution options, betting that patience pays off in share versus peers who "aren't being surgical."

## The read — 3-5 points from the whole transcript
1. **Growth is self-help, not macro.** With PMI at 48.6 and the "industrial economy... essentially flat," CEO-adjacent leadership was blunt that this quarter's double-digit growth "came with the same number of selling days" and reflects execution and share gains, not any tailwind — a clean signal that industrial demand itself has not turned yet.
2. **Pricing goal cut in real time, but framed as a positive.** Full-year like-for-like pricing guidance was lowered from 5%-8% to 3.5%-5.5%, and Q3's 240-270bps came in below plan because management chose to delay the price step ~30 days to "have better discussions with our customers" and find substitution options — Florness explicitly frames the miss as *better* execution, not slippage.
3. **The bonus reset was a real management-discipline miss.** SG&A ran ahead of plan because a stock-split math error (a factor-of-two mistake caught by a colleague mid-call) combined with underestimated new pay-program payouts meant district managers were overpaid by roughly "$8,000 a month too much" — a rare, candid admission of an internal control gap that "really matters for '26."
4. **A structural blind spot in their own reporting just got fixed — and it was bigger than they thought.** Twenty years into tracking OEM vs. MRO mix by "guesstimating," better systems revealed OEM is actually a larger share globally than the domestic proxy suggested, and Florness admits he'd have guessed OEM was ~30% of sales six months ago versus what the new data shows — a candid case of a Fortune 500 industrial company not truly knowing its own revenue mix.
5. **Inventory-as-investment thesis, explicitly prioritized over dividends.** Florness argues added fastener inventory earns a better return than a dividend and states plainly that companies sitting on excess cash "usually destroy shareholder value" — a clear capital-allocation philosophy statement, not throwaway color.

- **Mexico/OEM-heavy geography is the weak spot**: Fastenal's Americas-ex-US business, skewed toward OEM fasteners, is "having a tough year" — direct evidence that manufacturing-heavy end markets are hurting more than MRO/diversified ones.
- Not a consumer-facing business; omitted from that angle, but the mix shift toward healthcare, education, government, and warehousing/logistics customers is effectively a defensive diversification away from cyclical manufacturing exposure.

## The business — what's working, what's not
- **Working: large-account penetration.** Sites spending $10K+/month grew 8.1%, $50K+/month ("on-site-like") grew 15.4%; national account sales were up double digits, and a 20-year customer just expanded from two sites to five, making Fastenal its primary national supplier.
- **Working: fastener restocking initiative.** A "company-wide effort... across sales, supply chains, and operations" that fixed an inventory-model mistake (branches had gotten "too tied" on inventory and were losing share) drove +15% fastener growth in September and a real gross-margin lift.
- **Working: digital flywheel.** FMI + e-business now 61.3% of total sales (vs. prior-year levels), with FMI daily sales growth near 18% — well above company average, though e-business growth (8%) is explicitly called out as underwhelming pending a fastenal.com relaunch.
- **Not working: cost discipline lagged growth.** The bonus/SG&A reset was large enough to warrant a dedicated Q&A exchange and an admitted math error live on the call; management flags this "really matters for '26," not a one-quarter issue.

## Investing & scarcity
- **Capex stepping up**: guided to $235-255M for 2025 vs. $214M in 2024, driven by FMI devices, the Utah and Atlanta distribution hubs, automated picking, IT/digital projects, and vehicles.
- **Inventory build is a deliberate investment, not a cash-management drag**: inventories up 10.5%, partly to "improve product availability" and partly "we accelerated some inventory schedules for future delivery into current periods ahead of tariffs."
- **The binding constraint is policy/legal uncertainty, not physical scarcity**: "we have limited visibility and share our customers' uncertainty over how current trade policy may impact demand" — tariff litigation outcomes, not supply availability, are what's gating decisions.
- **Capital allocation philosophy stated explicitly**: inventory investment is preferred to dividends when it earns a return — "if we're adding inventory, and it's enhancing our returns, we will continue to add inventory... that's a better use of excess cash than even than a dividend."

## Where they think it's going vs what they're doing about it
- **Believe**: 2026 brings a demand inflection once tariff/trade policy "calms down" and customers can plan with known cost structures again — "the real challenge is the fatigue on pricing," implying the pause is temporary.
- **Act**: They did raise prices in August despite preferring not to, and are actively rerouting supply chains around tariffs (e.g., diverting shipments through Canada's West Coast at ~8% higher cost to avoid a larger US tariff) — real, funded actions taken now rather than waiting for policy clarity.
- **Gap flagged**: Management is simultaneously guiding to *lower* pricing impact for Q4 while acknowledging costs "are continuing to rise" and warning of a possible fresh margin squeeze — the optimism about calming trade policy is not yet reflected in their own near-term cost outlook, a genuine tension the CFO and CEO both flagged unprompted.
